# Section 1: What ALS Is

Q: What is ALS?

Context: Users new to the field, including students, ask what the disease is before asking about the data.

A: Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), sometimes called Lou Gehrig's disease, is a progressive neurological disease that attacks the nerve cells (motor neurons) that control voluntary muscles. Both upper and lower motor neurons degenerate, so muscles weaken and waste away over time. (Source: NINDS, National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke.)

---

Q: What are some key facts and figures about ALS?

Context: Users ask for general statistics. Quote only these figures and name the source.

A:
- About 90% of cases are sporadic (no family history) and about 10% are familial. (Source: NINDS.)
- Most people with ALS live about 2 to 5 years after symptoms begin; about 10% live 10 years or more. (Source: NINDS.)
- In the United States, the CDC National ALS Registry estimated about 24,800 adults were living with ALS in 2017 (range 17,800 to 31,843). ALS was most common in people aged 60 to 69. (Source: CDC National ALS Registry, 2017 prevalence report.)
- Changes in several genes are linked to ALS, including C9orf72 and SOD1. The Neuromine datasets include genetic data; see the Answer ALS Data Catalogue and ALS TDI ARC Data Catalogue.

For more detail or newer figures, see the NINDS ALS page (ninds.nih.gov) or the CDC National ALS Registry (cdc.gov/als). Do not quote other statistics (for example lifetime risk) unless they are added to this document.
